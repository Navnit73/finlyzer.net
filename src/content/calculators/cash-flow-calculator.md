# How to Find Net Cash Flow

Net cash flow is the change in a business's cash over a period. It tells you whether more money came in than went out, and by how much.

**Quick answer:** Find the cash flow from operating, investing and financing activities, then add them together. A positive result means cash grew during the period; a negative result means it shrank.

## Net Cash Flow Formula

**Net cash flow = operating cash flow + investing cash flow + financing cash flow**

Each of the three figures is its own inflows minus its outflows, so any of them can be negative. You can check the answer with the balance method:

**Net cash flow = cash at end of period − cash at beginning of period**

If the two methods disagree, a transaction is missing or sits in the wrong section.

## Net Cash Flow Example

The example loaded in the calculator is one month for a small business that starts with 25,000.00 in the bank.

| Activity | Inflows | Outflows | Cash flow |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Operating | 49,000.00 | 41,500.00 | +7,500.00 |
| Investing | 2,000.00 | 9,500.00 | −7,500.00 |
| Financing | 10,000.00 | 5,400.00 | +4,600.00 |
| **Net cash flow** | | | **+4,600.00** |

Cash rises from 25,000.00 to 29,600.00. The business generated cash from operations, spent all of it on new equipment, and the new loan is what actually increased the balance.

# How to Find Operating Cash Flow

Operating cash flow (OCF), also called cash flow from operations, is the cash the day-to-day business generates: selling, buying stock, paying staff, rent and tax. It is the most important of the three numbers because it shows whether the business funds itself.

There are two ways to calculate it. They use different inputs but give the same result.

## Operating Cash Flow Formula: Direct Method

**Operating cash flow = cash received from customers + other operating receipts − cash paid to suppliers, employees and for operating expenses − interest paid − income taxes paid**

The direct method is the natural choice for small businesses and anyone working from bank statements, because every number is a real deposit or payment.

### Direct Method Example

| Line | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash received from customers | 48,500.00 |
| Other operating receipts | 500.00 |
| Paid to suppliers and for inventory | −18,200.00 |
| Payroll and wages | −14,600.00 |
| Rent, insurance, advertising and other costs | −6,250.00 |
| Interest paid | −350.00 |
| Income taxes paid | −2,100.00 |
| **Operating cash flow** | **7,500.00** |

## Operating Cash Flow Formula: Indirect Method

**Operating cash flow = net income + non-cash expenses ± changes in working capital**

The indirect method starts from profit and removes everything that is not cash. Most published cash flow statements use it, because it explains why profit and cash differ.

### Working Capital Adjustments

Calculate each change as the closing balance minus the opening balance, then apply this rule.

| Balance sheet item | If it increased | If it decreased |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts receivable | Subtract | Add |
| Inventory | Subtract | Add |
| Prepaid expenses | Subtract | Add |
| Accounts payable | Add | Subtract |
| Accrued expenses | Add | Subtract |

An increase in receivables means sales were booked but not yet collected, so cash is lower than profit. An increase in payables means costs were booked but not yet paid, so cash is higher than profit.

### Indirect Method Example

| Line | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Net income | 6,200.00 |
| Add: depreciation and amortisation | 1,400.00 |
| Less: increase in accounts receivable | −2,300.00 |
| Add: decrease in inventory | 800.00 |
| Add: increase in accounts payable | 1,100.00 |
| Add: increase in accrued expenses | 300.00 |
| **Operating cash flow** | **7,500.00** |

Switch the calculator to the indirect method and you get the same 7,500.00 as the direct method.

## Operating Cash Flow vs Net Income

Net income and operating cash flow drift apart for three reasons: non-cash expenses (depreciation reduces profit but not cash), timing (credit sales and unpaid bills) and inventory (stock bought is cash spent before it becomes a cost). If operating cash flow is consistently below net income, look at how fast customers pay and how much stock you hold.

# Cash Flow From Investing and Financing Activities

## How to Calculate Cash Flow From Investing Activities

**Investing cash flow = proceeds from selling assets and investments − capital expenditures − purchases of investments**

Investing activities cover long-term assets: equipment, vehicles, property, capitalised software, and securities or loans to others. A negative figure is normal for a growing business, because it means the business is buying assets.

## How to Calculate Cash Flow From Financing Activities

**Financing cash flow = new borrowing + shares issued and owner contributions − loan principal repayments − dividends and owner drawings**

Financing activities are transactions with lenders and owners. Only loan principal belongs here under US GAAP; interest paid stays in operating cash flow. For a sole trader or partnership, owner drawings are a financing outflow.

### Where Common Items Belong

| Transaction | Section |
|---|---|
| Customer payments, supplier payments, wages, rent | Operating |
| Interest paid, income tax paid | Operating (US GAAP) |
| Buying or selling equipment, vehicles, property | Investing |
| Buying or selling shares and bonds held as investments | Investing |
| New loan received, loan principal repaid | Financing |
| Owner contributions, dividends, owner drawings | Financing |
| Transfers between your own bank accounts | Not a cash flow |

# How to Find Free Cash Flow

**Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capital expenditures**

Free cash flow is the cash left after paying for the assets the business needs. In the example, operating cash flow of 7,500.00 minus capital expenditures of 9,500.00 gives free cash flow of −2,000.00: the month's equipment purchase was funded by the new loan, not by operations.

# What Your Net Cash Flow Tells You

The sign of each section says more than the total.

| Operating | Investing | Financing | What it usually means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive | Negative | Negative | Healthy: operations fund investment and repay lenders or owners |
| Positive | Negative | Positive | Growing: investing more than operations can fund alone |
| Negative | Positive | Positive | Under pressure: selling assets and borrowing to cover operations |
| Negative | Negative | Positive | Early stage: funded by loans or owners while the business builds up |

One period rarely tells the whole story. Run the calculator for several months or quarters and watch the trend in operating cash flow.

# Turn a Bank Statement Into a Cash Flow Report

Your bank statement already lists every receipt and payment for the direct method. To use it:

1. **Total the money in and money out.** Upload the statement to the [bank statement analyzer](/analyzer/bank-statement-analyzer) to get the totals and a breakdown of spending.
2. **Sort the lines by activity.** [Convert the statement to Excel](/convert/bank-statement-to-excel) and tag each row as operating, investing or financing.
3. **Remove transfers.** Movements between your own accounts change neither section.
4. **Enter the totals above.** Use the statement's opening balance as cash at the beginning of the period, and check that the calculated ending cash matches the closing balance.

If the ending cash does not match, [reconcile the statement with your books](/reconcile/bank-reconciliation-tool) to find the missing or duplicated entries.
