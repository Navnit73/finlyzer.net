# Compound Interest Formula

**A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t)**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Balance at the end |
| P | Starting amount (principal) |
| r | Yearly interest rate as a decimal: 5% = 0.05 |
| n | Times interest is added each year: 1, 2, 4, 12 or 365 |
| t | Years |

The interest earned is **A − P**. When interest is added continuously, the formula becomes **A = P × e^(r × t)**.

## With a Deposit Every Period

**A = P × (1 + i)^N + C × ((1 + i)^N − 1) ÷ i**

Here C is the deposit, i = r ÷ n is the rate per period and N = n × t is the number of periods. That assumes each deposit is made at the end of a period. If you pay in at the start, multiply the deposit part by (1 + i).

If you pay in monthly but the account compounds quarterly or daily, the periods don't line up. The calculator then turns the rate into its effective annual rate, **(1 + r/n)^n − 1**, and from that into a rate for each month.

# Daily, Monthly or Yearly Compounding: How Much Does It Change?

10,000 left for 10 years at an example rate of 5%, with no further deposits:

| Interest added | Balance after 10 years | Interest earned | APY |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Once a year | 16,288.95 | 6,288.95 | 5.000% |
| Twice a year | 16,386.16 | 6,386.16 | 5.063% |
| Quarterly | 16,436.19 | 6,436.19 | 5.095% |
| Monthly | 16,470.09 | 6,470.09 | 5.116% |
| Daily | 16,486.65 | 6,486.65 | 5.127% |
| Continuously | 16,487.21 | 6,487.21 | 5.127% |

Going from yearly to monthly adds 181.14. Going from monthly to daily adds 16.56. Compounding frequency matters most at high rates and over long periods; for a savings account, a higher rate beats a more frequent schedule.

# What 200 a Month Grows Into

This is the example loaded in the calculator: 10,000 to start, 200 paid in at the end of each month, an example 7% compounded monthly, for 20 years.

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Starting amount | 10,000.00 |
| 240 deposits of 200 | 48,000.00 |
| Interest | 86,572.72 |
| **Balance after 20 years** | **144,572.72** |

The first 10 years earn 20,713.58 of interest. The second 10 years earn 65,859.14, more than three times as much, because interest is now being paid on interest. Money left to compound for longest does most of the work, which is why starting earlier usually beats saving more later.

# Rule of 72: How Long Until Your Money Doubles?

Divide 72 by the yearly rate to estimate how many years a lump sum takes to double. The exact answer, with yearly compounding, is ln(2) ÷ ln(1 + r):

| Rate | Rule of 72 | Exact |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2% | 36.0 years | 35.0 years |
| 4% | 18.0 years | 17.7 years |
| 6% | 12.0 years | 11.9 years |
| 8% | 9.0 years | 9.0 years |
| 10% | 7.2 years | 7.3 years |
| 12% | 6.0 years | 6.1 years |

The shortcut is closest around 8%. The calculator shows the exact figure for your rate and compounding.

# APY, AER and the Effective Annual Rate

Banks advertise two numbers. The **nominal rate** is the yearly rate before compounding. The **effective rate** includes compounding: APY in the United States, AER in the United Kingdom, or simply the effective annual rate. A 5% rate compounded monthly has an effective rate of 5.116%.

To compare accounts, use the effective rate. To model an account in this calculator, enter the nominal rate with its compounding frequency, or enter the APY with yearly compounding; both give the same balance.

# When Compounding Works Against You

The same maths applies to debt. Unpaid credit card interest is added to the balance, and next month's interest is charged on it. That is why a card balance left alone grows quickly. To see the cost of a card balance and how long it takes to clear, use the [credit card interest calculator](/calculators/credit-card-interest-calculator). Compound interest also differs from flat interest on the original amount, which the [simple interest calculator](/calculators/simple-interest-calculator) covers.
